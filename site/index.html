The ground cannot power your sensors.It can host them.

There is a persistent hope that the Earth is quietly offering free electricity — that a copper rod, an antenna coil, a resonance in the sky might run a landscape full of instruments with no batteries to change. The hope is off by three to nine orders of magnitude, and the measurements that kill it are the same ones that show what actually works.

Atmospheric electricity pays about 0.6 nanowatts per square metre. Soil bacteria pay three milliwatts. Sunlight pays twenty watts.

But a sensor that needs no power at all changes the question. A resonator has no key, no state and no compute — it answers an interrogation and costs nothing to keep alive for thirty years. Put the energy in one node that moves, and the landscape fills with instruments that cannot go flat.

The comparison that matters

A square metre of collector at three metres, in perfect fair weather, with a perfect electrometer and no leakage, gathers about half a nanowatt. A square metre of soil under it, colonised by the bacteria that were already there, gives roughly half a million times more — and keeps giving at night, under canopy, and through snow, which is exactly when photovoltaics stop.

Plate IIIElectroculturethe evidence splits

The copper coil, and the forty kilovolts

Electroculture is two different claims wearing one name. One has been tested properly and does not survive. The other is real, has a living research descendant, and runs on a power supply.

The gap between them is about ten thousand. A hobbyist copper-coil antenna is a 1924 Imperial College apparatus with the high-voltage supply removed, sold on the premise that the atmosphere replaces it. The atmosphere offers a four-volt ceiling and delivers two millivolts.

Plate IVTeslafive claims, five verdicts

Right about the circuit, wrong about the source

Tesla's 1901 radiant-energy patent describes harvest, accumulate, threshold-dump — precisely the topology every microwatt sensor node uses today. He got the power management right a century early and attached it to a source that pays nanowatts.

The claims have to be separated, because they have different truth values and the strong ones keep getting used to prop up the weak ones.

The near-miss that keeps doing damage

Tesla estimated an Earth fundamental near 6 Hz from lightning-return patterns. Schumann computed 7.83 Hz in 1952. The numbers are close because both scale off the Earth's circumference — but Tesla's mechanism was a longitudinal wave reflecting at the antipode, and Schumann's is a transverse mode of the Earth–ionosphere cavity. Different cavity, different physics, different wave. A coincidence in the first significant figure is not a confirmation, and treating it as one is the single most common error in this entire literature.

Instruments that outlive the plan that funded them

Climate adaptation fails at maintenance. A monitoring network installed with a grant dies in year three when the batteries go and nobody is paid to walk the line. Passive elements do not have that failure mode: the failure mode of a resonator is that it is still a resonator.

Plate VIArchitecturefour layers

Concentrate the energy, distribute the silence

The architecture that survives every budget on this page has one powered thing and many silent ones. It removes the battery from every point that is hard to reach by accepting one battery somewhere that is not.

Soil attenuation is a security feature

A passive resonator cannot authenticate. Any element cut to the same frequency answers the same interrogation identically, so authenticity has to come from controlling the volume you interrogate rather than from cryptography. Underground, that control is nearly free: thirty centimetres of read range in wet soil is a far stronger physical bound on who could be answering than anything free space gives you. The property that makes buried radio hard is the same property that makes buried radio trustworthy.

What is not verified here

  • Sources are read, not replicated. Everything below is cited from published work; only the electroculture trial was read in full. Treat the numbers as supported, not verified — the distinction is the whole point of Plate VII.
  • Two circulating claims are deliberately absent. A "University of Copenhagen tomato trial, 2018–2020" and a "2022 citizen-science trial of 47 gardeners, +1.8 % ± 14.3 %" appear in search summaries sourced to content farms. No primary source exists for either. They are probably fabricated and should not be propagated.
  • Commonly repeated, unconfirmed: that the UK Electro-Culture Committee (1918–c.1936) wound up after failing to replicate. Widely cited, not verified here.
  • Original and untested: the suggestion that lightning sferics or 1 MW VLF transmitters could serve as free illuminators for passive resonators. Sferic impulses are roughly ten thousand times stronger than the Schumann continuum and are impulses, which is what rings a high-Q element — but the return path obeys the same link budget, which probably kills it. Cheap to falsify; experiment D is the nearest relative.
